01

What Was Missing

Richard Chen is the CFO of Harmon. He's been on the board for six years and has approved every cybersecurity budget without fully understanding a single one. Not because he was careless—but because every CISO report he's ever received was written for someone with a CISSP certification. He trusted the room. He signed.

Last quarter, he approved a $420K cybersecurity budget in four minutes. He hasn't slept well since.

Under NIS2, SEC disclosure rules, and DORA—intent is no longer enough. Neither is trust.

Board members are now individually required to prove what they knew, what they asked, and why they believed the risk was acceptable.

Richard followed the process that worked for decades. The regulators want something different now: a live document of every decision. Visible. Defensible. Traceable.

Standard tools—SecurityScorecard, CyberSaint, Vanta—report to security teams. They were built for analysts, not board oversight. None can prove a director engaged with a specific decision at a specific moment on a specific day.

The law changed. The tools didn't. Every existing governance process lives in gray territory.

Vigil is the first tool designed specifically to operate outside that gray. Every status update, every budget request, every moment a director individually considered a decision, captured in real time, timestamped, attributed, immutable.

Vigil doesn't replace the paperwork. It makes gray territory architecturally impossible.

When investigators ask what Richard knew, what he considered, and when—Vigil answers with a record that was never capable of being anything other than true.

02

The Calls I Made

01
Stability, Not Growth

The hardest constraint in Vigil isn't engineering. It's evidence of board oversight. If Vigil can't prove the board understood the risk before they acted, then the product has already failed.

Instead of simplifying a cybersecurity dashboard, I started looking for a different visual language entirely. The first round had 10+ directions — Sales Analysis, Operational Efficiency, Target vs. Growth. Every one of them shared the same grammar: progress toward a goal. Security doesn't work that way. It needs stability, not growth.

The second round narrowed to visuals where stability is the normal state. A medical monitor had the right urgency hierarchy — but Richard isn't used to reading one. A stock chart is his native language. He's read one every Monday morning for thirty years. The candlestick carried the right grammar but risked one question: "Is this a stock?" I chose the mountain. Same time-based structure. That question disappears.

In the third round, I added two threshold lines — 74% and 49%. Above 74%: stable, no action required. Between 74% and 49%: something has shifted — the security team is managing it, with or without a budget request. Below 49%: high-risk, immediate board attention required. But one risk remained.

Richard only logs in quarterly — and between reviews, the visual grammar fades. A board member who hasn't opened Vigil in six weeks shouldn't need to remember what 74% means. Two things make that possible.

The first is a footnote beneath the mountain. One line of plain language under every state: "No board action required." Or: "Board oversight required." The mountain shows the shape. The footnote names it.

The second is the status signal. Traffic light logic was the obvious choice — every person in that boardroom learned it before they learned to drive. But the default traffic light has two distinct accessibility failures — and each required a different fix.

Red-green color blindness affects 8% of the population, collapsing the most critical signal distinction into indistinguishable territory. I replaced green with Intense Azure (#0071E3), red with Deep Crimson (#960018) — removing the problematic pairing without losing the traffic light logic the board already understands. Amber was a different problem. Standard yellow fails WCAG contrast requirements on light backgrounds. I replaced it with Mellow Brown (#B36B00) — contrast grounds alone.

I anchored the surrounding interface to two neutrals — Sonic Silver (#767676) and light gray (#EDEDED) — so the status colors never have to compete with anything for attention.

The mountain communicates posture to a board member in seconds. Every operational metric — CVE counts, patch compliance rates, incident timelines, mean time to detect — that didn't answer a governance question lives one click away. A security analyst opening Vigil would find it thin. Anyone who needs the full picture, it's there. As evidence.

02
Machines Can't Testify

For data input, the obvious move is automation: pull live data from security dashboards, convert metrics into business impact, feed it to the mountain. JSON makes it easy. JSON makes it fast.

JSON also makes it unaccountable.

If a cybersecurity platform flags a false positive and the mountain turns amber, every board member in that room will ask the CISO for an explanation. Now the CISO has to defend a machine's output instead of their own judgment. And if the CISO is defending a system's mistake rather than their own decision, that record won't survive legal scrutiny. A tool designed for evidence can't depend on systems that deflect accountability.

So I chose manual input. Not because automation is wrong. Because evidence requires accountability—and machines can't testify.

Manual input means the CISO doesn't explain a system's mistake. They explain their judgment. Every submission is timestamped. Every choice is recorded. They're not fighting automated noise. They're making decisions. And those decisions are now evidence.

03
One Forced Structure

Cybersecurity investments come in infinite varieties: efficiency gains, gap remediation, insurance requirements, regulatory deadlines, threat response. The CISO recognizes their weight in seconds. Boards don't.

One forced structure solves it.

Three categories. Maximum three. Not because three is magic. Because three matches the mountain's traffic light logic from Call-1: Intense Azure (Blue), Mellow Brown (Amber), Deep Crimson (Red). The board already reads this language. Using it here eliminates a second learning curve.

When the CISO submits a budget request, they must choose one:

Blue (Strategic): A calm situation. The CISO is claiming this is building, not reacting. Improves efficiency, builds capability, strengthens resilience, or advances long-term posture.

Amber (Priority): The middle territory. The organization isn't under attack. The cybersecurity team is in full control — they know the weak spots well. Addresses an active gap, insurance requirement, or near-term risk.

Red (Critical): Survival challenge. Active attack (ransomware, DDoS, data breach), immediate threat, or regulatory deadline. It has two distinct scenarios.

Scenario-1: Regulatory deadline. Unlike insurance requirements, regulatory deadlines aren't negotiable. They're career-defining. The CISO can't minimize them.

Scenario-2: Active attack. Revenue stops immediately. Customer transactions halt. Employees stop working. The company enters operational paralysis. Everyone's mind shifts to survival mode.

The boardroom changes tone. They are not judging the CISO's requested amount. They are asking: "Can we operate tomorrow?" "Are customer funds safe?" "How long until markets react?"

Approving budget becomes non-optional. The board needs answers in seconds.

If a board member has to scroll to find the requested amount, I haven't reduced cognitive load. I've added it.

Blue and Amber show the budget request after details. Red needs to show the requested amount first. But a different structure in a crisis doesn't reduce load — it adds more.

So I kept Red's structure identical to Blue and Amber. Same form. Same radio buttons. Request amount, ROI, and priority level in 32px text — always at the top.

In the real world, cyberattacks unfold in 3-5 phases. Phase 1-2 is stopping the bleeding. The CISO uses emergency funds and wastes zero time on approval. Vigil goes offline. It doesn't change the fact even if Vigil runs on a separate server. The CISO is no longer discussing "vulnerabilities." They are discussing operational survivability, business continuity, public trust.

So I didn't design anything for that situation.

Phases 3-5 are calmer. That's when the CISO reports what happened and what else they need. Vigil's back. That's where they use it.

04
Political Liability by Design

A record that can't be edited, deleted, or backdated after submission. It's a double-edged sword. It's not a shield.

So why would anyone use it?

One breach. Three perspectives. Each one facing the same record from a different side.

Elena Vasquez is 61. She was planning to retire at 63. Nine years ago she invested $2.3M in Harmon. Since then her stake had grown to $3.2M — the number she and her husband had worked out on a yellow legal pad at their kitchen table. The house in Lisbon they'd already looked at twice. The plan was close.

On a Tuesday, Harmon suffered a breach. By Thursday her position had collapsed 34%. By the following Monday she understood that 63 was no longer a number — it was a door that had quietly closed.

She didn't cry. She got very still. And then she hired a lawyer.

Four weeks before that Tuesday, Marcus Sterling — CISO of Harmon — found it. Filed a remediation request. $340K. Specific gap. Specifically said: "This is the one I'm worried about."

The board had approved $420K in cybersecurity spend just last quarter. They declined the $340K. The minutes listed the risk as manageable.

The breach happened in the vendor authentication layer. Exactly where Marcus said it would.

Elena's lawsuit wasn't anger. It was the only language left.

Her lawyer's first question was simple: "What did the board know — and when?"

Richard is in a conference room with his lawyer, reviewing subpoenaed records.

"Do you have anything showing what you knew and when?"

Richard opens Vigil. A declined red request. Filed by Marcus Sterling. Registered as an immediate threat. He saw it. He clicked "Noted" — timestamped, attributed, immutable, documented in real time by a system that was never capable of being anything other than true.

But his lawyer pulled three years of data. Eleven investment requests total. The last four: all red. AI efficiency improvement. Registered red. Insurance requirement. Registered red. System upgrade. Registered red. Immediate threat. Registered red.

Marcus used red so many times that red stopped meaning anything. Richard didn't ignore the warning. He'd been trained to absorb it.

The same record Elena's lawyer is holding. Three people. Different positions.

Vigil doesn't choose sides. It doesn't protect the board. It doesn't protect the CISO.

Both walk away when both acted reasonably. Neither walks away when they didn't.

It's not a shield. It's not a sword.

It's just a mirror.

03

The Rules I
Designed By

Rule 01
The record is append-only. No entry can be edited, deleted, or backdated after submission.
Rule 02
The CISO controls the narrative. No word in the CISO's submission may be generated, suggested, or altered by the system.
Rule 03
The system controls the signal. One red anywhere — insurance, legal, budget — makes the mountain red. One amber with no red makes the mountain amber. Every input blue, the mountain is blue.
Rule 04
Submission is irreversible. A submitted record can only be superseded — never replaced or removed.
Rule 05
Every submission is named. The CISO submits under their own identity. No intermediary, proxy, or system agent may create a record on their behalf.
Rule 06
No comment, like, or vote may exist in the system. Vigil documents decisions. It does not shape them.
Rule 07
Budget release happens on paper. Vigil captures engagement. It does not authorize spend. That boundary cannot move.
Rule 08
Red uses the same structure as blue and amber. No layout change, modal, or mode switch may be introduced for any status state.
Rule 09
Every pixel is there for a legal reason. Nothing decorative. Nothing that suggests this was built for convenience. This was built for court.
Rule 10
The status note is system-generated and immutable. No human may edit the plain-language statement attached to a signal score.
Rule 11
The record of a rejected budget request is permanent. A rejection cannot be hidden, archived, or removed from the board view.
Rule 12
Post-meeting documentation is CISO-only. After a board meeting, only the CISO may upload supporting evidence — notes, PDFs, audio, video — to the record.

Gave Up

"Vigil is not designed for comfort. Every record exists for court, not conversation. "

" The board has two options: View Full Document and Noted. Noted exist as pre-meeting session. Only the CISO can upload discussion after a decision. Any question a board member kept to themselves, any doubt they never voiced, vanishes instantly. It cannot be presented as evidence in court. "

05

What Shifted

Three weeks into designing Vigil, I almost deleted everything.

I had a working prototype. An automated governance instrument. It gives directors cyber risk visibility in a language they already speak — so they can understand risk without technical expertise.

Then I stress-tested it.

The CISO's first question stopped everything. "Who's liable when it's wrong? If Vigil translates a red alert as amber and the board doesn't escalate, and a breach happens, did the tool fail or did I? I spent 15 years building credibility in that room. My name is on the governance report, not Vigil's."

The CFO wasn't gentler. "You're telling me a JSON feed translated into a dashboard is going to convince my board? They don't trust dashboards. They trust people."

I had looked at a communication failure and followed the noise. I thought directors would understand cyber risk flawlessly. That security analysts would waste zero time on jargon translation. That clarity was the problem.

But I had broken something more important than clarity. I had broken trust.

If I added an override function, the CISO would have to verify it. The board would have to ask the CISO before reacting. It was worse than a slide deck the CISO prepared himself.

I had the feeling of someone who'd broken a working system trying to fix a problem that wasn't there. Does this deserve to exist? Why did I start it?

Then I made a decision. Twenty-four hours. That's how long I'd look for a reason. If I couldn't find one, Vigil was gone.

Within 14 minutes I found it. It had been in front of me the whole time. I was too deep in translation to see it.

The legal landscape for board members had shifted. Not gradually. Structurally. Board members can now be personally banned from leadership if they can't prove they exercised cyber oversight. Intent is no longer enough. Neither is trust.

I hadn't designed a security tool. I had designed a governance instrument. And no tool existed yet as evidence of board oversight.

Designing something that doesn't exist yet. That was the point from the beginning.
